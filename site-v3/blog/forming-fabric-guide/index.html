What Is a Forming Fabric (Forming Wire)? A Practical Guide

What a forming fabric (forming wire) is, layer constructions, mesh count and CFM explained, and how to choose — a practical guide from Aurora Filtech.

What is a forming fabric?

A forming fabric is the woven monofilament belt that forms and dewaters the fiber web at the wet end of a paper machine or nonwoven forming line. Stock sprays onto the fabric; water drains through its openings while the fibers stay on top to become the sheet. It is still commonly called a forming wire — or simply the wire — because early machines ran on woven bronze wires. Polyester monofilament replaced bronze decades ago, but crews kept the name: forming fabrics, commonly called a wire.

Forming fabric vs forming wire vs paper machine clothing

Three names, three levels. Forming fabric is today's standard product name — the one used across the major suppliers' catalogues. Forming wire is the trade nickname for the same product. Paper machine clothing (PMC) is the umbrella category: forming fabrics, press fabrics and dryer fabrics together. When you order, order by function (forming), construction (layers) and specification (mesh count, CFM) — not by nickname.

How a forming fabric works

The fabric does three jobs at once: it drains water through calibrated openings, it supports and carries the fragile fiber web, and it transmits drive force over the foils, vacuum boxes and couch roll without marking the sheet. Everything about its design — yarn diameter, weave pattern, layer count — is a trade-off between drainage speed and fiber retention.

Layer constructions: 1-layer, 1.5-layer, 2-layer, 3-layer

  • 1-layer — maximum drainage and caliper stability for fast machines and heavy stock.
  • 1.5-layer — extra CD yarns for better retention with most of the drainage kept.
  • 2-layer — smoother sheet side for printing and packaging grades.
  • 3-layer (SSB, triple layer) — separate sheet side and machine side: fine top for formation, coarse bottom for stability. The default for quality grades.

Mesh count and air permeability (CFM)

Mesh count is the number of MD and CD yarns per centimetre — more yarns mean smaller openings and better retention. Air permeability (CFM) measures how much air the fabric passes at a standard pressure; suppliers match CFM to your stock, speed and vacuum design. A fabric that is too open loses fiber and fines; one that is too tight chokes drainage and loads the vacuum system.

How to choose a forming fabric

  • Start from the machine: former type, speed, and vacuum layout.
  • Then the product: packaging, printing & writing, tissue, or nonwoven web.
  • Then the failure history: blinding calls for monofilament and higher CFM; fiber loss calls for finer counts or multi-layer.
  • Always specify seams and edges to the machine's design — pin, clipper or endless.
